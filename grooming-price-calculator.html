Why the hourly rate matters more than the price

Two groomers can both charge $70 for a cocker spaniel and one of them is earning half as much. If the job takes 1.5 hours, that is $47 an hour. If the dog is a matted, double-coated 70-pounder that eats three hours, the same $70 is $23 an hour — below what a shop assistant costs once you count product, water, heating, insurance and the empty slot you could not fill.

The fix is not always a higher price. It is usually pricing by the time the dog takes rather than by the breed name on the phone, and then writing that time down when the booking is made, so the day can be planned around it.

The four numbers behind any grooming price

  1. Time — the real one, including drying. A bath and brush on a husky is mostly drying time, and drying time is the part everyone under-quotes.
  2. Consumables — shampoo, conditioner, cotton, blades, sharpening. Typically $3–6 per dog; more for medicated or de-shedding work.
  3. Overhead per hour — rent, insurance, power, water, software, divided by the hours you actually groom. A one-chair room at $900/month on 100 grooming hours is $9 an hour before anything else.
  4. Your target hourly rate — what you need to take home. Everything above is a cost; this is the reason the shop exists.

Put together: price = (time × (target rate + overhead per hour)) + consumables. A 1.5-hour cocker at a $40 target with $9 overhead and $5 product is 1.5 × 49 + 5 = $78. If the market will not pay $78, the answer is not to charge $60 — it is to shorten the job (a tidy instead of a full groom), or to fill the empty hours with something that pays better.

Five pricing decisions that are free to change today

  • Charge for the matted dog, not for the breed. Dematting is skilled, slow, uncomfortable work. A per-15-minute line item is easier for the owner to accept than a mystery surcharge.
  • Price the nails separately. It is the highest-margin five minutes in the shop and the easiest walk-in to convert.
  • Quote a range on the phone, confirm at the door. A range protects you from the dog nobody described accurately; a fixed quote transfers that risk to you.
  • Charge a no-show deposit on new clients. One skipped Saturday is worth more than the deposit ever will be — the deposit exists to prevent the skip.
  • Raise prices for new clients only. Existing clients keep their rate; new bookings start at the new one. Nobody's invoice changes without a conversation.

Questions

Is this calculator free to use and share?

Yes. No signup, no email, nothing is stored or sent — the numbers are calculated in your browser and disappear when you close the tab. Print the rate card, hand it to a new groomer, put it on the wall.

Why does the price change so much with coat type?

Because time does. A short-coated beagle and a double-coated husky are the same weight and nowhere near the same job: one is a 45-minute bath and towel dry, the other is a 2-hour blow-out that fills the room with hair. Coat multiplies time, and time is the cost.

Should I charge more for doodles?

Usually yes, and not because of fashion. Curly coats hold water, mat at the skin, and need scissor work that cannot be rushed. In the calculator a doodle coat adds 30% to the base — which for a medium dog turns a $60–85 full groom into roughly $78–110, and adds about 25 minutes on the table.

What if my market will not support these prices?

Then the constraint is capacity, not price: raise the number of dogs you can hold per day, or move the mix toward higher-margin services (nails, tidies, de-shedding). Both need one thing — knowing at booking time how long each dog takes, which is exactly what the day sheet is for.

Where do the numbers come from?

Published US price guides and public shop rate cards for 2025–2026, cross-checked against the ranges groomers report in trade communities. They are averages for independent shops; mobile and city-centre pricing runs higher, rural lower. Nothing is scraped from any single company's price list.
