7-day plan

Set up your grooming day book in seven days

Most solo groomers don't lose bookings because they're bad at grooming — they lose them because the book is in three places at once: a paper diary, a phone calendar and a notebook of client notes. This is a one-week plan to fix that. One small task a day, 15 to 30 minutes each, nothing to sign up for.

Day by day

1

List every client and pet

20–30 minutes

Go through your phone, your messages and your paper diary and write one line per pet: owner name, phone, pet name, breed, and roughly how often they come in. Don't clean it up yet — just get everyone out of your head and into one place.

Why first: every other day depends on this list, and the list itself is the thing you currently don't have.

2

Write down what you actually charge

15–25 minutes

For each service — full groom, bath and tidy, bath and brush, de-shed, puppy first visit — note the price you charged the last three times. You'll usually find two or three prices for the same job. Pick one and write it down.

If you're not sure what to charge, the price calculator turns size, coat and service into a range plus the hours the job really takes.

3

Block one normal week

20 minutes

Open a blank week and fill it as if it were a real one: grooming slots, the gap for drying, lunch, the school run, the last appointment that always overruns. Be honest about how many dogs fit in a day — most solo groomers overestimate by one.

A schedule you can't keep is worse than no schedule. One honest week becomes the template you repeat.

4

Add vaccination dates

20–30 minutes

For each pet, write the rabies expiry date, and bordetella and DHPP if you require them. Then mark which ones fall in the next 30 days. This is the step most paper books skip, and the one that causes the awkward conversation at the door.

Some states and many salons require current rabies before a dog is on the table. A date you can see beats a certificate in a drawer.

5

Set up reminders

15 minutes

Write one reminder message you're happy to send — short, warm, with the rebooking interval built in ("Rocco is due around the 14th, want the usual slot?"). Then send it to the three clients who are closest to due.

Re-bookings are the cheapest bookings you'll ever get. You don't need a marketing system, you need one sentence and a habit.

6

Group multi-pet households

15 minutes

Two dogs from one family should sit together: same phone number, same address, same vaccine requirements, one visit. Merge the duplicate entries you created on Day 1 — most groomers find two or three.

Households are how you stop re-typing a phone number every time a second dog is added, and how you see the family's whole day at once.

7

Back it up and close the loop

15 minutes

If it's paper: photograph the pages, or keep a duplicate in a second place. If it's an app: check that the data lives somewhere you can restore from, and that it isn't behind an account you don't control. Then print a fresh week and put it where you work.

The day book is the business. Losing it is the one failure that costs a season, not an afternoon.

Questions

Do I need an app to do this?

No. The plan works on paper, in a spreadsheet or in any calendar. The app simply keeps the seven pieces in one place and adds the vaccine warnings and reminder text for you.

How long does the whole week take?

About two and a half hours in total, spread over seven days. Days 1 and 4 are the longest at 20–30 minutes; the rest are 15 minutes.

What if I already have a book that works?

Then keep it. The only steps worth borrowing are Day 4 (vaccination dates with a 30-day warning) and Day 6 (grouping pets by household) — those are the two that paper books most often miss.

Why seven days instead of one afternoon?

Because a book built in one sitting gets abandoned in a week. One task a day, each small enough to finish, is what makes the habit stick.
